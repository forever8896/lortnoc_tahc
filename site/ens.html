ENS v2 · Permissioned Resolver

A name you own, with permissions you control.

Every record separately permissioned. Enforced on-chain, revoked in one transaction, without asking us for anything.

Why we built on it

An account a company can delete was never yours.

Two things had to be true at once. A user’s identity has to survive us — if we disappear or are ordered to close an account, the name and the keys people reach them by keep working. And the infrastructure operating that account has to hold the narrowest possible power, in a form the user can inspect and withdraw.

A row in our database is a claim about our future behaviour. We needed a permission that is a public, checkable fact. It’s also the business model: handles are what we sell, and that only works if a handle is genuinely the buyer’s.

How it works here

  1. Own resolver per account. Claiming a handle deploys a VerifiableFactory proxy of PermissionedResolverImpl — their contract, not a row in a shared one.
  2. Records carry the routing. pubkey, walrus, inbox, stealth, knock.
  3. Delegate exactly one record. authorizeTextRoles(name, "eth.lortnoc.inbox", gateway, true). Our gateway rotates the inbox pointer and is reverted on everything else.
  4. Revoke without us. Same call, grant=false. One transaction, the user’s signature.
  5. Prove it’s genuine. verifyContract(proxy) returns the implementation — checkable without trusting our backend.
  6. Unlinkable issuance. Gated on an unspent zk nullifier, claim relayed so payer ≠ claimer.

For the ENS team

v2 didn’t just add features to v1. It made the registry pluggable, and our entire identity layer hangs off that one decision.

IRegistry with a different ownership model

Being able to slot our own registry under lortnoc.eth and have it issue names on a rule that isn’t “this wallet paid” is the reason handles can be unlinkable to payment. In v1 that thought isn’t expressible — you’d be bolting policy onto a system whose ownership model already disagreed with you.

Per-record roles, resourced by keccak256(node, part)

This is the detail that makes least-privilege real rather than aspirational. Our gateway holds write access to exactly one text key and is reverted on every other. Not “shouldn’t” — reverts, on-chain, every time.

Demonstrating that revert live is the best thirty seconds of our demo, and it’s entirely your primitive doing the work.

verifyContract and TokenRegenerated

Two things that only exist because someone thought past the happy path. verifyContract means a counterparty can confirm a resolver came from the canonical factory without trusting us at all. And bumping the token version on a role change, so revocation invalidates stale approvals, is the kind of detail that comes from asking what happens after a revoke rather than during one.

One sharp edge, since you’ll want to know we actually used it

grantRoles reverts on the resolver; the authorize*Roles wrappers are the way in. Cost us an afternoon before we read the source. Worth documenting louder — it’s the first thing anyone reaching for EAC will try.

Discoverability is a dial, not a switch

Because every handle resolves through a CCIP-Read gateway, real code runs on each lookup and can answer differently per record and per caller. Five rungs, held in one text record:

GhostNot findable. Only someone handed the handle directly can reach them.
UnlistedResolvable if known. Not searchable, not reverse-linkable.
KnownFindable only by someone who already holds an identifier for them.
SearchableAppears in people-search, opt-in.
PublicFully linkable, deliberately.

The gate is the creative part, not the index behind it. Roles govern writes; this ladder is a read-time policy at the gateway — a separate mechanism, deliberately.
