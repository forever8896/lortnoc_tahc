Sui · Walrus · Seal

A vault we can’t open, on storage we don’t own.

Every conversation is batched, encrypted under keys derived on your device, and written to a decentralized network that has no idea what it is holding. The rule for who may decrypt it runs on-chain, where you can read it for yourself.

Why it’s essential

If we hold your messages, we can be made to hand them over.

The threat model was never only Chat Control. It is every future order served on whoever runs this service. A privacy product that keeps a readable copy of your conversations has relocated the surveillance, not removed it — and it has made itself the single most attractive thing in the system to compel.

So the constraint is absolute: we never hold plaintext, and we never hold a key that opens it. That rules out our own servers. It also rules out ordinary cloud storage with encryption we administer, because “we promise not to look” is a policy, and policies change the moment a subpoena arrives.

What that leaves is a specific pair of requirements: storage that cannot read what it stores, and an access rule that nobody — including us — can quietly edit. Walrus answers the first. Seal answers the second.

Why Seal, rather than just encrypting it ourselves

We could encrypt with our own keys and tell you the rules. Seal replaces that promise with a program. Decryption is gated by seal_approve, a Move function on Sui — the access policy is public code anyone can read and verify, not a conditional buried in a backend only we can see.

It is the difference between they say only you can read it and here is the rule, check it yourself. For a product whose entire claim is that you don’t have to trust us, that distinction is the product.

How it works here

  1. Batch. Messages are grouped with Quilt before anything else. Chat messages are tiny, and writing them one at a time to a blob store is enormously wasteful — batching is roughly 100–420× cheaper at this size. Storage economics decide whether a privacy product is affordable enough to actually run.
  2. Encrypt. Seal encrypts the batch with threshold encryption, so no single party holds the key that opens it.
  3. Store. The encrypted blob goes to Walrus, which erasure-codes it into shards spread across the network. Any sufficient subset reconstructs the blob, and the network self-heals as nodes come and go. No node holds anything meaningful, and no node can read what it holds.
  4. Point. A Sui object, ConversationHead, is the mutable pointer — { participants, headBlobId, seq, updatedAt }. Blobs are immutable; the head is what moves as the conversation grows.
  5. Index. Your ENS text record eth.lortnoc.walrus points at a Manifest blob listing your conversations, so any device holding your master secret can rebuild your entire inbox from nothing but your handle.
  6. Read. Resolve the handle → manifest → heads → blobs → decrypt with id_seal, derived from your master secret through HKDF. Client-side, always. Reading your own history never requires our permission and is always free.

Why not 0G Storage

We evaluated it seriously. Using it would have collapsed storage onto the same EVM spine as the rest of the stack, which is architecturally tidy and worth wanting.

We deferred it for three reasons. There is no threshold-encryption equivalent with an on-chain access policy — which is precisely the property doing the work above. Walrus has the more battle-tested durability story for “your data, portable and durable.” And there is no Quilt-style small-object batching, which matters disproportionately for chat-sized writes.

0G earns its place in this stack elsewhere, on the problem it is actually the right answer to. That work is described on the compute page.

What we don’t claim

Walrus is a durable log, not a realtime bus. Delivery in this build is polling. A realtime relay is roadmap; we would rather say that than describe latency we haven’t shipped.

The conversation head is single-writer for now. Two participants writing the same head concurrently would contend on seq. Multi-writer ordering is post-hackathon work.

The access policy runs in its fallback form. Today the gateway issues a short-lived Seal session key against a valid zero-knowledge proof. The stronger design — checking the nullifier inside seal_approve against an on-chain registry — is specified and is the next step, not what is running as you read this.
